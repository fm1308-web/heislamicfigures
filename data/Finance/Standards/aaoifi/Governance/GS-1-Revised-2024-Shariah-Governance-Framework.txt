Preface

PR1       Since its inception, the Islamic finance industry has experienced remarkable growth, attracting diverse groups of stakeholders and investors, ranging from individuals and institutions to governments.

PR2       The industry’s rapid growth and impact highlight the critical need for a sound and effective Shari’ah governance framework. Such a framework is necessary to ensure that Islamic financial institutions (IFIs / the institutions) continue to conform closely to Shari’ah principles and rules in all their operations and activities and fulfil their fiduciary responsibilities towards all stakeholders in a fair and transparent manner.

PR3       This necessity stems from the fact that Islamic finance is based on Shari’ah principles and rules, and that any deviation or non-compliance, as well as, non-fulfilment of fiduciary responsibilities may undermine the integrity of the industry. This may result in a loss of public trust and confidence and eventually hamper the industry’s growth in the long run.

PR4       This standard, along with a set of complementing standards, establishes and explains the minimum requirements and best practices for a Shari’ah governance framework.

PR5       This standard serves as a centre-point for the overall Shari’ah governance requirements covered in multiple governance standards and guidance issued by AAOIFI from time to time.

Introduction

Overview
IN1        This standard requires the implementation of a Shari’ah governance framework by IFIs, where and to the extent applicable, other institutions including social finance institutions operating in line with Shari’ah principles and rules.

IN2        This standard outlines the core principles to establish the overall framework of Shari’ah governance for such institutions. It also defines the roles and responsibilities of, and expectations from, various stakeholders and the functions and organs of governance and management with regard to the implementation of such principles. In addition, it provides detailed guidance in respect of such functions and organs for which a separate AAOIFI Governance Standard (GS) does not exist.

Rationale(s) for issuing this standard
IN3        The issuance of this standard is prompted by the need to:

a.           establish the core principles of and develop an overarching framework for Shari’ah governance that is comprehensive, appropriately flexible, accommodating and adaptable to the evolving complexities of the Islamic finance industry; and

b.           promote uniform Shari’ah governance best practices that can be implemented globally, promoting cross-border compatibility and facilitating international transactions.

AAOIFI Governance Standard (GS) 1 (Revised 2024)
Shari’ah Governance Framework

Objectives of the standard

1.           The objectives of this standard, along with the complementing AAOIFI Governance Standards (GSs)1, are to:

a.           improve the level of Shari’ah compliance in the Islamic finance industry;

b.           improve the quality and integrity of Shari’ah governance while optimising the costs by providing comprehensive guidance on minimum and best practices for various organs of Shari’ah governance;

c.           help regulatory and supervisory authorities (RSAs) and Islamic financial institutions (IFIs / the institutions) to fulfil their fiduciary responsibilities towards various stakeholders with regard to Shari’ah compliance and governance;

d.           provide greater harmonisation of Shari’ah governance structures and practices across jurisdictions, particularly as the number of institutions with cross-border operations increase;

e.           provide a higher level of transparency in Shari’ah compliance of an institution by standardising and streamlining the process of issuing and implementing Shari’ah rulings and auditing the implementation of the same;

f.            enhance public confidence in the Shari’ah compliance of the institutions and reduce the reputational risk of Shari’ah non-compliance through improved Shari’ah governance structure, greater transparency and more effective accountability; and

g.           complement other standards issued by AAOIFI2.

Scope

2.           This standard applies to all IFIs, including Islamic finance windows, across all sectors. RSAs may choose to apply this standard to other institutions in their jurisdictions as appropriate. This standard shall also be applied insofar as practicable by the institutions other than IFIs adopting AAOIFI GSs,
including social finance institutions.

Principles of Shari’ah governance

Principle 1: Shari’ah governance framework
5.           An institution shall have in place a comprehensive Shari’ah governance framework which encompasses a set of institutional and / or system-wide mechanisms to ensure effective and independent oversight of Shari’ah compliance of its products, services, processes and business operations.

Principle 2: Role of regulatory and supervisory authorities
6.           The regulatory and supervisory authorities (RSAs) shall have an adequate understanding of Shari’ah non-compliance risks and accordingly ensure that the institutions have in place an adequate and effective Shari’ah governance framework and reporting mechanism.

Principle 3: Central Shari’ah board
7.           RSAs and / or governments are recommended to establish and appropriately empower central Shari’ah boards15 (CSB) in order to harmonise Islamic banking and finance practices in the jurisdiction in line with Shari’ah principles and rules.

Principle 4: Unique organs of institutional Shari’ah governance framework
8.           The Shari’ah governance framework shall consist, at a minimum, of the following components /
organs:

a.           Shari’ah supervisory board (SSB);

b.           Shari’ah compliance function;

c.           internal Shari’ah audit function; and d.           external Shari’ah audit (ESA).
Principle 5: Role of those charged with governance and senior management
9.           An institution shall clearly define the roles and responsibilities of those charged with governance (TCWG), senior management and other organs in upholding the Shari’ah compliance of its activities and operations.

Principle 6: Shari’ah supervisory board
10.        An institution shall have in place an independent, competent, empowered and adequately resourced
Shari’ah supervisory board (SSB).

Principle 7: Shari’ah compliance function
11.        An institution shall have in place a Shari’ah compliance function that is competent, empowered and adequately resourced to discharge its duties.

Principle 8: Internal Shari’ah audit function
12.        An institution shall have in place an internal Shari’ah audit function that is competent, empowered and adequately resourced to discharge its duties.

Principle 11: Islamic finance subsidiaries / associates
15.        An Islamic finance subsidiary / associate of a conventional financial institution shall have a suitable Shari’ah governance framework (see paragraph 5) in place to ensure effective Shari’ah compliance of its operations.

17.        The provisions of this standard may be suitably implemented using the proportionality criteria and as per its relevance to different sectors of the Islamic finance industry.

Principle 14: Transparency and disclosure
18.        An institution shall be transparent in its Shari’ah governance to its shareholders, customers, other relevant stakeholders and market participants.

Principle 1: Shari’ah governance framework

“An institution shall have in place a comprehensive Shari’ah governance framework which encompasses a set of institutional and / or system-wide mechanisms to ensure effective and independent oversight of Shari’ah
compliance of its products, services, processes and business operations.” (Paragraph 5)

19.        A Shari’ah governance framework refers to a set of institutional and / or system-wide arrangements through which RSA / institution ensures that there is effective and independent oversight of Shari’ah compliance of the institution’s products / services, processes and business operations. This means that there exists a governance framework which facilitates an institution to put in place measures to comply with Shari’ah principles and rules in all its activities.

20.        A Shari’ah governance framework shall include arrangements to incorporate effective and independent oversight of Shari’ah compliance of the products / services offered by the institution.


12
This entails, among other things, having in place effective procedures to design, structure and market these products / services while ensuring Shari’ah compliance at all times. Additionally, Shari’ah governance extends to systems, processes, policies, procedures and codes of conduct, as well as, people. Other important aspects include training the staff concerned and creating an overall culture of Shari’ah compliance. In particular, there should be no violations of Shari’ah standards or ethical values while selling and marketing products and dealing with customers.

21.        Since the institutions market themselves to be Shari’ah compliant, attracting, in particular, Shari’ah conscious customers, it is of paramount importance that the institutions are not only Shari’ah compliant in their operations, dealings and transactions, but are also perceived to be Shari’ah compliant by their stakeholders, including the general public. A major incidence of Shari’ah non- compliance, or repeated failures in Shari’ah governance, can result in financial and reputational loss to an institution. This may also have consumer protection and risk management implications for the jurisdiction (such as loss of consumer confidence and / or higher operational risk due to Shari’ah
non-compliance). In a worst-case scenario, it can lead to a systemic loss of confidence in all institutions, causing financial stability concerns. A sound Shari’ah governance framework, on the other hand, helps an institution to discharge its fiduciary and other responsibilities towards its stakeholders (including the general public) and leads to enhanced stakeholder confidence in its integrity and operations.

22.        The main objectives of a Shari’ah governance framework for an institution are defined in line with the objectives of this standard as prescribed in paragraph 1.

Principle 2: Role of regulatory and supervisory authorities (RSAs)

“The regulatory and supervisory authorities (RSAs) shall have an adequate understanding of Shari’ah non- compliance risks and accordingly ensure that the institutions have in place an adequate and effective
Shari’ah governance framework and reporting mechanism.” (Paragraph 6)

23.        It is the responsibility of the RSAs to ensure effective Shari’ah governance of the institutions in their jurisdiction. RSAs are expected to play their role in establishing and overseeing a robust Shari’ah governance framework.

24.        RSAs shall allow Shari’ah compliant financial services to be offered only by licensed and / or authorised IFIs, including Islamic finance windows. [Explanation: In case of Islamic finance windows / Shari’ah compliant financial services offered by conventional financial institutions, this principle shall be applied in line with the requirements of the respective AAOIFI GS16.]

25.        RSAs are strongly recommended to develop suitable prudential guidelines and requirements for the implementation of Shari’ah governance frameworks in order to manage Shari’ah non-compliance risk by the institutions. RSAs shall consider adding suitable criteria enabling adequate Shari’ah governance (including Islamic finance knowledge, qualifications and experience) to complement the standard fit and proper criteria for the management and control positions of the institutions. RSAs
shall ensure that the interests of the shareholders, investment accountholders (IAHs) and other fund
providers of the institutions are safeguarded, so that their funds, which have been collected with a

Principle 4: Unique organs of institutional Shari’ah governance framework

“The Shari’ah governance framework shall consist, at a minimum, of the following components / organs:

a.           Shari’ah supervisory board (SSB);

b.           Shari’ah compliance function;

c.           internal Shari’ah audit function; and

d.           external Shari’ah audit19 (ESA).” (Paragraph 8)

31.        SSBs have been the most common way to seek Shari’ah-related opinions for an institution. SSBs include Shari’ah scholars who are engaged professionally by the institution to provide guidance and supervision, including on Shari’ah rulings related to products / services, transactions, operations, etc. (see paragraph 4(m)). To guide the day-to-day operations from a Shari’ah compliance perspective,
an individual with Shari’ah background and knowledge is also considered necessary by most institutions as the head of Shari’ah compliance department20 (SCD) (in line with the requirements of respective AAOIFI GS21).

32.        The Shari’ah compliance function provides a mechanism for ensuring the compliance of an institution’s financial arrangements, contracts and transactions with Shari’ah principles and rules. It is an integral part of an institution’s governance and control structure. SCD means a department (and includes a division, team, section or unit) entrusted with the responsibilities of Shari’ah compliance in accordance with the requirements of the respective AAOIFI GS22.

33.        The internal Shari’ah audit function is part of the governance organs of an institution, established in line with the requirements of the respective AAOIFI GS23, being independent of management, with the primary objective to provide assurance to TCWG and the SSB in relation to the institution’s adherence to Shari’ah principles and rules. Internal Shari’ah audit assesses the adequacy and effectiveness of intended controls pertaining to adherence to Shari’ah guidelines, whereas the Shari’ah compliance function is an ongoing process of monitoring the institution’s overall activities and Shari’ah compliance environment.

34.        The ESA provides an independent third-party assurance to the stakeholders of an institution about its compliance with Shari’ah principles and rules. ESA has become a good-practice globally and is increasingly considered essential for safeguarding the integrity, reputation and sustainability of an
institution, especially those categorised as public interest institutions.

Principle 5: Role of those charged with governance and senior management

“An institution shall clearly define the roles and responsibilities of those charged with governance (TCWG), senior management and other organs in upholding the Shari’ah compliance of its activities and operations.”
(Paragraph 9)

35.        A comprehensive Shari’ah governance framework demands the creation of certain new organs of governance and management and the assigning of additional responsibilities to existing organs. It is important to note that Shari’ah compliance is the responsibility of each stakeholder of an institution and cannot simply be delegated to a few (for example, the SSB).

Those charged with governance (TCWG)
Roles and responsibilities of TCWG
36.        TCWG of an institution (mostly referred to as the board of directors (BOD), along with its respective committees, particularly the audit and governance committee / audit committee) has an oversight responsibility to ensure that an effective and robust Shari’ah compliance environment and culture exist in the institution. They are responsible for establishing a good governance and control framework for the institution, developing a strategy for compliance with Shari’ah principles and rules, and oversight of the management in implementing the same and in achieving the desired objectives. TCWG may use any of their dedicated committees to assist for the purpose of fulfilling their responsibilities under this standard.

37.        TCWG have the ultimate responsibility of institutionalising a robust Shari’ah governance framework at the institution that is commensurate with the size, complexity and nature of the business (to be comparable with other governance and control functions). In fulfilling this responsibility, they shall:

a.           approve policies relating to governance structure and reporting arrangements, Shari’ah non- compliance risk management and other areas that are material to the effective implementation of Shari’ah governance within the institution;

b.           oversee the implementation of the CSB’s rulings (if any) and decisions, rulings and guidance of the SSB within all business and functional lines, including any business or risk implications arising from such implementation;

c.           oversee the implementation of the internal control framework (including ensuring the independence of Shari’ah compliance and internal Shari’ah audit functions) to prevent systematic Shari’ah non-compliance and any rectification measures to resolve incidences or circumstances of Shari’ah non-compliance or that may result in Shari’ah non-compliance;

d.           oversee the performance of senior management and other officers entrusted to implement the Shari’ah governance framework such that they are satisfied that the measures of their performance are aligned with Shari’ah governance objectives; and

Appendix B: Basis for conclusions

Adopting principle-based or rule-based standard development approach
BC1       The AAOIFI Governance and Ethics Board (AGEB / the board) discussed as to whether this standard, along with the set of complementing standards in respect of Shari’ah governance framework, should be principle-based or rule-based. Two possible options and their respective pros and cons were evaluated. It was discussed that the principle-based standards provide guidelines and objectives while allowing flexibility for interpretation, considering the varying circumstances faced by institutions across different markets. These also provide a reasonable level of flexibility in the hands of the regulatory and supervisory authorities (RSAs) to address the specificities of their respective jurisdictions. Such flexibility is likely to encourage more RSAs to adopt and implement AAOIFI Governance Standards (GSs). On the other hand, the rule-based approach ensures the utmost quality of the governance and control processes and a greater level of standardisation, consistency and harmonisation in the industry, within and across jurisdictions. However, such approach results in specific stipulations and detailed prerequisites, leaving limited room for judgment and adaptability.

BC2       The board decided that the standards shall be principle-based. However, in certain aspects, the principles need to be defined in appropriate detail (i.e., prescriptive approach for
defining principles). The board further considered that at times there is a thin line between a principle and a rule, particularly, where issues of Shari’ah compliance, fairness, justice or equity arise, which does not leave significant room for interpretation and / or flexibility. Accordingly, it was decided that, whilst the standard will be principle-based, a more prescriptive approach will be adopted, where appropriate, to ensure consistency, provide comparability and reduce the ambiguity for decision-makers in various Islamic financial institutions (the institutions / IFIs) and, where applicable, across jurisdictions.

BC3       The board members agreed that adopting principle-based standards for Islamic finance enables greater adaptability, innovation, global applicability and use of professional judgment. These factors contribute to the overall development and growth of the Islamic finance industry, allowing it to better serve the needs of stakeholders. The board members also agreed that there is often no “one-size fits all” model for Shari’ah governance as legislations and regulatory structures vary across jurisdictions and reflect local markets’ historical development. The board further noted the significance of the principle of proportionality and agreed to incorporate the same in the standard to support this approach.

Defining Shari’ah principles and rules
BC4       The definition of Shari’ah principles and rules (including the hierarchy and the order of priority) was introduced for the first time in the GS 9 “Shari’ah Compliance Function” that was issued in 2018. The hierarchy was discussed extensively by the members of AAOIFI Governance and Ethics Board (AGEB), the working group and the Shari’ah committee. It was
also discussed during several public hearings.





30
BC5       There was a detailed discussion on the need for a centralised Shari’ah principles and rules hierarchy and the importance of defining it. AGEB felt the need to explain and elaborate on their firm viewpoint of including the Shari’ah principles and rules hierarchy in the relevant AAOIFI Standards.

BC6       Some stakeholders during the public consultation stage were of the view that the Shari’ah principles and rules hierarchy should not be defined at all to encourage Shari’ah scholars’ Ijtihad and in order not to limit product innovation in the Islamic finance industry. Certain stakeholders were of the view that this matter should be left to the respective RSAs and they should be allowed to define their own Shari’ah principles and rules hierarchy to
maintain consistency within their specific jurisdiction. AGEB’s viewpoint was to clearly define the Shari’ah principles and rules hierarchy given that no credible Shari’ah governance framework can be established unless the Shari’ah principles and rules hierarchy is defined, which will ultimately ensure global uniformity. In addition, the clarification of Shari’ah principles and rules is essential to provide and implement the necessary controls which in turn help the implementation of a credible Shari’ah governance framework.

BC7       After due deliberations, the board agreed that it is essential to clearly define Shari’ah
principles and rules in the body text of the relevant AAOIFI GSs.

BC8       It is worth noting that the requirements of the applicable AAOIFI Financial Accounting
Standards (FASs), as mentioned in the Shari’ah principles and rules hierarchy (See paragraph
4(I), iv), are well defined in Chapter 7 of “AAOIFI Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting” (revised 2020) which clarifies the importance of determining the Shari’ah principles and rules hierarchy.

BC9       The board concluded that it is necessary in due course to develop a standard on “Governance Principles for Determination and Application of Shari’ah Principles and Rules” to establish a clear and formal approach to ascertaining a set of Shari’ah principles and rules applicable to IFIs.

RSAs’ responsibilities in respect of the Shari’ah governance framework
BC10     The board discussed whether it would be appropriate to outline certain responsibilities of the RSAs in this standard which are crucial for having an effective and robust Shari’ah governance framework in a jurisdiction. The board noted that generally the governance requirements of AAOIFI GSs are meant for the industry and not the RSAs. However, in this case as an exception, the board considered it essential to include RSAs’ respective responsibilities in the standard and address their role. This is because RSAs are one of the most important external stakeholders of the IFIs, entrusted to safeguard the interests of the stakeholders at large and maintain the industry’s long-term stability. This would contribute to providing clarity, and hence, can significantly reduce confusion among stakeholders. Accordingly, it was decided to include in the principles, the responsibilities of RSAs (see paragraphs 23-29) in this standard. In addition, the board noted that defining the role of RSAs will ensure having effective and independent oversight of the Shari’ah governance
systems, promote transparency and, in turn, enhance public confidence in the industry.




31
The balanced approach of the standard – optimising the costs
BC11     While deciding the overall approach towards the development of the standard (and other standards within the project), the board had a significant question to address, i.e., how to keep a balance between the quality of the Shari’ah governance framework and the respective cost to the institutions. On the one hand, the overall project to develop the standard (along with other standards within the project) was aimed at improving the quality and integrity of Shari’ah governance frameworks in place across the industry. On the other hand, there were concerns raised by the members of the board and the working group, particularly those representing the industry players, about the additional costs including time, efforts and resources involved in bringing about the proposed improvements in the Shari’ah governance framework. Similar concerns were raised during the public consultation stage as well.

BC12     The board deliberated at length over the need to consider the optimisation of cost without compromising the quality of the standard. After due deliberations, the board agreed to follow a cost vs. benefit analysis approach in multiple aspects and selected the outcome in each case that aimed to improve the quality and integrity of Shari’ah governance while optimising the costs. The board also agreed that the cost optimisation approach should be part of the overall objectives of the standard (and other standards within the project).

BC13     Such cost optimisation was ensured through, e.g., introducing the proportionality principle (i.e., application according to the size, complexity and risk profile of the respective IFIs), maintaining several requirements as optional or preferred instead of mandatory and allowing transition time for certain requirements.

External Shari’ah audit – mandatory for public interest institutions after a period of three years and the correlation with AAOIFI SOAA 6
BC14     The working group and the board discussed various significant questions regarding external
Shari’ah audit (ESA), including the following:

a.           is there a need for having ESA, as one of the organs of Shari’ah governance in addition to the SSB, Shari’ah compliance and internal Shari’ah audit?

b.           if yes, then should ESA be mandatory for all IFIs?

c.           if yes, then what would be the right time to make it mandatory, considering market readiness and availability of the right set of expertise?

d.           is there a need to keep minimum requirements related to ESA in this standard, considering that the AAOIFI Standard on Auditing and Assurance (SOAA) 6 “External Shari’ah Audit” provides detailed guidance about this and encourages having ESA performed for all IFIs?

BC15     The board discussed these questions at length, their potential impact on the standard and on the overall Shari’ah governance framework prescribed by the board. After due deliberations, the board concluded as follows:




32
a.           ESA is an integral organ of Shari’ah governance as it is the only organ of governance that is completely independent of the management, as well as the institution and whose work is not subject to self-review threat;

b.           while principally, ESA should be mandatory for all IFIs, considering the costs and efforts needed and applying the principle of proportionality, it was decided that it shall be applicable only on public interest institutions (e.g., retail and / or listed institutions). For all other institutions, it shall remain recommended;

c.           considering the factors related to market readiness and availability of the right set of expertise in the external audit fraternity for performing external Shari’ah audit or availability of unique external Shari’ah audit firms, in different jurisdictions, a reasonable time period should be allowed (i.e., three years from the effective date
of this standard) before it becomes mandatory for public interest institutions. Earlier adoption remains recommended; and

d.           while AAOIFI SOAA 6 “External Shari’ah Audit” provides detailed guidance which applies to the external Shari’ah auditors and encourages ESA for all IFIs, it is important to provide the minimum requirements related to this function in this standard. The reason is that it is a governance requirement to conduct an audit, and the scope of an AAOIFI SOAA is to define how an audit or assurance exercise shall be conducted by the auditor; the same does not apply to the institution itself.

Applicability of the Shari’ah governance framework on overseas branches of
IFIs
BC16     A comment received during the public hearings sought clarification regarding the application of overall Shari’ah governance framework on the overseas branches of IFIs.

BC17     The first issue raised was regarding the challenges in application of the Shari’ah governance framework in the absence of a normal corporate structure, unlike a corporate institution. The board was of the view that the overseas branches of an IFI normally function as full- fledged IFIs and hence these should be subject to the overall Shari’ah governance framework.

BC18     The second issue was regarding the application of the AAOIFI Shari’ah governance framework where the IFI and its overseas branches function in jurisdictions where RSAs’ approach towards adoption of AAOIFI GSs is different. For example, there can be situations where the IFI’s jurisdiction follows AAOIFI GSs, while the overseas branches operate in a jurisdiction where AAOIFI GSs are not applied, or vice versa.

BC19     After due deliberations, the board concluded that regarding the overseas branches, the respective RSA may provide guidance / relaxation about the framework’s applicability, considering the absence of the normal corporate structure (and certain governance organs). It was suggested that normal practices regarding the flexibility in the corporate governance
structure may be adopted for the application of the Shari’ah governance framework as well.





33
BC20     Regarding different approaches of the respective RSAs, the board concluded that if the IFI is subject to the requirements of AAOIFI GSs, its overseas branches, being a part of the IFI itself, must follow such requirements at a minimum (in addition to any other requirements
of the RSA of the jurisdiction where such branches operate). On the contrary, if the overseas branches operate in a jurisdiction where AAOIFI GSs are adopted, the same shall be applicable to the extent of such overseas branches only (subject to specific guidance / relaxation by the RSA). It was also considered that the same analogy may suitably be adopted for the overseas subsidiaries of IFIs.

Establishment of central Shari’ah boards (CSBs) – level of emphasis of the recommendation
BC21     AAOIFI GS 8 “Central Shari’ah Board” encourages and recommends the formation of CSBs.
While developing this standard, it was considered relevant by the board to enhance the level of emphasis of such recommendation by making it “strongly” recommended (although not mandatory), and hence the exposure draft of the standard proposed the same. This was considered as aligned with the hierarchy of Shari’ah principles and rules already defined in multiple AAOIFI GSs, and the board’s overall approach towards bringing uniformity of Shari’ah principles and rules globally and within jurisdictions.

BC22     Certain comments were received during the public consultation phase and the Public Interest Monitoring Consultative Committee (PIMCC) was also of the view to keep it as recommended only, as it may not suit all jurisdictions. This view was also supported by certain board members suggesting that different RSAs adopt a decentralised approach for their respective jurisdiction consciously and it will be difficult for them to accept a near- mandatory status of CSBs (as reflected by the phrase “strongly recommended”).

BC23     After due deliberations, the board decided to keep the establishment of CSB as a
“recommended” practice instead of “strongly recommended”.

Quality assurance of external Shari’ah auditors and other service providers
BC24     A public hearing comment was received asking for a mechanism to provide quality assurance for the independent external service providers for the IFIs, particularly for the external Shari’ah auditors. The board agreed with the significance of the comment in the overall context of the Shari’ah governance framework. However, it concluded that this matter may not be relevant to the scope of the current standard, and that generally it would be the responsibility of the respective RSAs to ensure the existence and effective implementation
of a framework for quality assurance for the independent external service providers for the IFIs, particularly for external Shari’ah auditors. The board noted that in different markets service providers on the conventional side are subject to similar mechanisms by the financial sector regulators or professional self-regulatory bodies, and a similar approach would be
acceptable in this case.

Appendix C: Brief history of the preparation of the standard

H1         AAOIFI and Islamic Financial Services Board (IFSB) agreed to collaborate and cater to the growing needs of the global Islamic finance industry, in various areas of mutual interest. One of the significant collaboration efforts was in the area of standardisation and harmonisation of Shari’ah governance practices across the globe. The two institutions collaborated to issue the exposure draft of “IFSB-AAOIFI Revised Shari’ah Governance Framework for Institutions Offering Islamic Financial Services” on 16 Ramadan 1444H, corresponding to 7 April 2022 after completing the due process.

H2         Based on extensive feedback received from stakeholders and considering the mandates of each organisation, both the institutions mutually agreed to separately review and, where necessary, issue relevant guidance / standards on Shari’ah governance within their respective areas of focus.

H3         AAOIFI Governance and Ethics Board (AGEB / the board) in its 32nd meeting on 12 Safar
1445H, corresponding to 28 August 2023, via video conferencing, approved the formation of a working group and the initiation of the development process of exposure drafts of six governance standards, primarily containing the requirements of the joint exposure draft, which were not covered elsewhere in the existing governance standards.

H4         Following the board’s approval, two working group meetings were held to discuss the draft standards on 25 Rabi’ II 1445H, corresponding to 9 November 2023 and 2 Jumada I 1445H, corresponding to 16 November 2023, respectively. The members of the working group agreed on conceptual issues and recommended the draft standards for the approval of AGEB.

H5         AGEB held its 34th meeting on 13-14 Jumada I 1445H, corresponding to 27-28 November
2023 in Bahrain, and approved the issuance of exposure drafts of six governance standards, including this standard. The board directed the secretariat to incorporate the necessary changes in light of the comments of the AGEB members and the discussions during the meeting. The board further approved a fast track due process for finalisation and issuance of these standards duly taking into account the effort and stakeholders’ feedback already incorporated in these standards.

H6         After incorporating the board’s comments and necessary changes, this exposure draft was
issued on 12 Jumada II 1445H, corresponding to 25 December 2023.
